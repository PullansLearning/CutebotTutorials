The robot is the course

Students already know how to snap blocks together. What is new here is that the program leaves the screen: it becomes a car on the floor that drives, signals, measures and obeys. There are 15 chapters across 6 levels, each about 45 minutes long, for grades 6–9 once they know micro:bit blocks.

There is no screen-only path. The MakeCode simulator does not model the Cutebot — not the motors, not the sonar, not the floor probes — so every chapter needs a real car. Plan the lesson around having the hardware in the room, not around having it later.

The rhythm

Every chapter runs in the same seven beats

Learn the rhythm once and you can teach any of the 15 chapters. Each beat has a job, and together they move a pair from watching to predicting to building to debugging.

🔥1

Warm-Up

The hook says what changes today, then one line about where this lives outside the classroom. The vocabulary for the chapter is rendered as cards, and every word carries an emoji.

Your move: Point at the emoji before you say the word. Most of your learners are reading English as a second or third language.
🤔2

Predict

Students read one program and choose, from three options, what the car will do. The answer and the reason are on the page.

Your move: This course has no simulator, so this is the only place students run a program in their heads. Take a whole-class vote before you reveal.
🙌3

Try It

Three or four short programs to download and run. Each one names a number to change, and the expected result is shown so nobody is stuck guessing.

Your move: Insist they change the number and download again. Cause and effect is the whole point here, not finishing first.
🗺️4

Plan

One paragraph saying what is being built, then three to five mission cards that split it up. From Level 4 on, a flowchart is added when the program has to make a choice.

Your move: Do not let them open the editor until a pair can say the plan out loud. The cards are a briefing, not code.
🔨5

Build

Numbered steps. Each step shows only the blocks it adds, so what is new stays visible, and the finished program comes last. Every chapter ends with a Drive Test on the floor.

Your move: Clear floor space before this section. The Drive Test is the result of the chapter — if you run out of time, cut something else.
🔧6

Fix It

A broken program beside the working one. The bug always changes what the car does; ugly-but-correct code is never the bug.

Your move: Ask what is different instead of pointing at it. The answer is in the Teacher Note on that chapter's page.
🚀7

Extend

Three tiers of extra projects. They give a goal, a flowchart and a picture of the result — never the finished code.

Your move: The plan for each task is in this chapter's teacher block, not on the student page. Send fast finishers to a red task rather than to the door.

In the room

Three things that look like a bug and are not

🔋

Weak batteries look exactly like wrong code

A tired set of AA cells makes the car crawl, pull to one side, or do nothing at all — the same symptoms as a bug. Change the batteries before you debug the program. Keep a charged spare set on the desk.

💡

A light threshold is a number for your room

Two chapters ask the car to react to brightness. The number that counts as dark depends on the lamps and windows in the room you are teaching in, so the lesson has students read the sensor first and choose their own. A number copied from another room will not work.

🛟

The robot moves, so the desk is a cliff

Cars drive off table edges and into feet. Work on the floor or on a taped-off area, know where the power switch is, and have students say what the car is about to do before they start it.

Where the code comes from

One chapter is one page of the ElecFreaks wiki

The lesson programs are not ours. They come from the ElecFreaks micro:bit Smart Cutebot wiki, the manufacturer's own documentation, which means ElecFreaks ran them on the real car before we taught them. The course covers 14 of those cases and adds one introduction chapter. Case 13 is left out: its receiving program is word for word the same as Case 12's, and it needs a joystick that is not in the kit — a new part rather than a new idea.

Where a lesson departs from the wiki program, the reason is written down and a check compares the printed code against the original on every build. That is also why the course is 15 chapters and not more: a chapter needs a wiki case behind it.

The extra work

Extend is where the depth is

Because the chapter count is fixed by the source, the room to stretch a strong pair is in the Extend projects. Those tasks are ours, not the wiki's, so the course deliberately does not hand out finished code for them — nobody has run it on a real car, and we will not claim it works. Students get the goal, a flowchart to plan against, and a picture of what success looks like. A pair that is properly stuck can open a folded hint with one or two key blocks in it.

Every Extend task's plan is printed in the teacher block at the bottom of that chapter's page, so you can see the intended shape without putting it in front of the class.

Checking understanding

Check-ups, attendance and progress

Each chapter has a matching check-up that is marked on screen the moment a student presses Finish. The pass mark is 70%, and results are reported across five areas: Concept · Reading · Debug · Building · Talk. Answers are never on the student's page.

Attendance and results are kept per learner on this computer and shown on the progress page, which is also where a student signs in. Nothing is sent anywhere unless a check-up result is mailed on purpose.

After school

Pacing — one chapter, one session

Micro:bit with Cutebot is built for after-school sessions that complete and extend what class time reaches. One chapter is one session, and its check-up closes it. A session is about 45 minutes. 📅One session a week
Level 1 takes 5 weeks; the whole course takes 15 weeks. At two sessions a week it takes 9 weeks.
➕Two or three sessions a week
Add a second course (a lane) instead of racing ahead. Going faster through one course thins it out. A lane keeps the extra time useful — for example Micro:bit STEM, Python STEM, Digital Citizen. The table shows which ones fit each level’s grade.
🔁Missed a session
Start the next session with the missed chapter’s Fix It and its check-up — about a quarter of a session — then join the class on the new chapter.
In one sentence: put the car on the floor early, let the pair predict before they download, let the Drive Test be the proof, and change the batteries before you blame the code.
